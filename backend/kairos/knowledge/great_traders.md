# Great traders and investors: how they actually worked

## What the great traders have in common
tags: great traders legends habits common traits market wizards masters lessons successful traders
Across very different styles (Buffett's decades-long holdings, Soros's macro bets, Simons's thousands of tiny statistical trades, Livermore's and O'Neil's breakouts), the same habits recur. They cut losses small and early and let winners run, so a minority of trades makes most of the money. They size positions by how sure they are and how much they can lose, never by hope. They have a written method they can state in a few sentences and stick to it, changing it only on evidence. They respect the market's trend and the big picture before the single stock. They keep cash, or wait, when nothing fits. And they review their mistakes honestly. Jack Schwager's interviews (Market Wizards) found no single winning method, but every winner had an edge they understood, strict risk control and a method suited to their own temperament.
**In Agora:** these habits are built into Kairos's desks: a stop on every trade, a 3 × ATR trailing stop, at most 5 open positions and 3 per sector, standing aside when a chart's entries lost on unseen trades (D-147), and Metis keeping only strategies that beat the index on years it never tuned on.

## Warren Buffett: buy wonderful businesses at fair prices and hold
tags: warren buffett berkshire value investing moat quality compounding long term hold intrinsic value circle of competence
Buffett buys whole businesses or their shares when they have a durable advantage (a "moat": brand, low cost, network, switching costs), honest and able management, high returns on capital without much debt, and a price below what the future cash flows are worth. He stays inside his "circle of competence" (businesses he understands), concentrates on his best ideas, and holds for years or decades, letting compounding work; his favourite holding period is "forever". He treats market drops as chances to buy ("be greedy when others are fearful") and keeps large cash reserves so he can. He avoids leverage, rarely sells, and ignores forecasts of the economy or the market. His edge is patience and temperament as much as analysis.
**In Agora:** Agora tests price-based rules, so Buffett's method can't be backtested directly (it needs company accounts). What carries over: the quality and low-volatility factor lists as a universe, buying strong companies on market-wide fear (index well below its 200-day average, India VIX high) rather than chasing, and holding winners with a wide trailing stop instead of booking small gains.

## Charlie Munger: few, great bets and avoiding stupidity
tags: charlie munger mental models inversion concentration quality patience avoid mistakes
Munger, Buffett's partner, pushed him from cheap "cigar-butt" stocks to great businesses at fair prices. His method: a latticework of mental models from many fields, "invert, always invert" (ask what would make the investment fail and avoid that), sitting on cash until a rare clear opportunity appears and then betting big, and owning very few holdings. He held that most of the gain comes from a handful of decisions over a lifetime, so the job is patience and avoiding big errors, not activity.
**In Agora:** inversion is the trade autopsy's question ("why did the losers lose?") and the stand-aside rule: when the evidence says a chart doesn't work, don't trade it. Fewer, stronger trades also pay fewer charges.

## Benjamin Graham: margin of safety
tags: benjamin graham margin of safety mr market value investing intelligent investor net net defensive investor
Graham, Buffett's teacher, taught that a share is part of a business, that "Mr. Market" offers prices every day that swing from euphoric to depressed and can be ignored or exploited, and that you buy only with a margin of safety, a price well below a conservative value, so that errors and bad luck still leave you whole. His defensive investor holds a diversified list of large, financially strong, dividend-paying companies bought at modest multiples; his enterprising investor buys very cheap stocks (even below net current assets) in a basket, since some will fail.
**In Agora:** the margin-of-safety idea maps to buying after large drawdowns with a defined stop, and to the cost-and-slippage cushion Metis demands before trusting a small edge.

## Peter Lynch: invest in what you know, by category
tags: peter lynch magellan invest in what you know ten bagger stalwart fast grower turnaround cyclical peg ratio
Lynch ran Fidelity Magellan from 1977 to 1990 with about 29% a year. He looked for companies he could understand from everyday life, then did the homework. He sorted stocks into slow growers, stalwarts, fast growers, cyclicals, turnarounds and asset plays, each with its own buy and sell logic: fast growers are held while growth lasts, cyclicals are bought when earnings are depressed and sold when they peak. He liked growth at a reasonable price (a P/E near or below the growth rate), owned many stocks, and let the few "ten-baggers" make the return. He warned against "diworsification" by companies and against trying to time the market.
**In Agora:** cyclicals map to sector rotation (metals, autos, banks turn with the economy: the sector page and the world desk), and "let the ten-baggers run" to the trailing stop over fixed targets.

## Philip Fisher and the scuttlebutt method
tags: philip fisher growth investing scuttlebutt quality management long term common stocks uncommon profits
Fisher bought outstanding growth companies (strong research, sales organisation, margins and management) after gathering "scuttlebutt" from customers, suppliers and competitors, and held them for very long periods; he said that if the job was done right when buying, the time to sell is almost never. He influenced Buffett's move toward quality.
**In Agora:** the long-hold idea fits the daily desks' wide trailing stops on strong trends; the research itself is outside a price-only lab.

## John Templeton: buy at the point of maximum pessimism
tags: john templeton contrarian global investing maximum pessimism bargain hunting crisis buying
Templeton bought what everyone else was selling, worldwide: in 1939 he bought every US stock under a dollar, and he went into Japan and emerging markets decades before others. His rule was to buy at the point of maximum pessimism and sell at maximum optimism, with a diversified basket, since some cheap things are cheap for a reason. He said the four most dangerous words in investing are "this time it's different".
**In Agora:** Kairos's news risk gauge and the market's fear readings (India VIX, VIX, breadth) mark pessimism; a strategy that buys strong stocks after a market-wide washout, with a stop, is the testable form.

## George Soros: reflexivity and big bets when right
tags: george soros reflexivity quantum fund macro boom bust pound 1992 conviction sizing
Soros's theory of reflexivity says prices don't just reflect fundamentals, they change them (rising shares let companies raise money cheaply, which improves the business, which lifts shares), so trends can feed on themselves into booms and then busts. He looked for those self-reinforcing trends and for the moment they become unsustainable. His famous 1992 bet against the British pound shows his style: form a thesis, test it with a small position, and when the market confirms it, go big. He was quick to admit errors and reverse; he said what mattered was not being right or wrong but how much you make when right and lose when wrong.
**In Agora:** "test small, add when confirmed" is pyramiding into a winner; reflexive booms show as strong momentum with rising breadth, which is why the momentum and trend families exist and why they need a trailing exit for the bust.

## Stanley Druckenmiller: liquidity, conviction and preserving capital
tags: stanley druckenmiller macro liquidity central banks concentration conviction capital preservation duquesne
Druckenmiller ran Duquesne for 30 years without a losing year and was Soros's lead manager in 1992. He watches liquidity and central banks above all ("earnings don't move the market, the Fed does"), looks 18 months ahead, and concentrates heavily when he has strong conviction with the trend and the macro in his favour. When he's wrong, or when he's lost his touch, he cuts fast and gets small; preserving capital comes before making money. He has said the key is to make a lot when you are right, not to be right often.
**In Agora:** Kairos's market backdrop (index trend, rates, the dollar, news gauge) is the liquidity view; the desks' smaller size after a losing day is his "get small when it isn't working".

## Paul Tudor Jones: defence first, the 200-day line
tags: paul tudor jones 200 day moving average defense risk reward macro trading 1987 losing position
Tudor Jones, who profited in the 1987 crash, puts defence first: he watches what he could lose, not what he could make, and will not average down on a losing trade ("losers average losers"). His simple trend rule is the 200-day moving average: stay out of, or reduce, anything trading below it. He looks for trades with at least 5-to-1 reward to risk, so even being right 20% of the time pays, and he cuts size when he's losing.
**In Agora:** the 200-day average trend filter is one of Metis's most tested universe and regime rules, and no Agora strategy averages down.

## Jim Simons and Renaissance: many small statistical edges
tags: jim simons renaissance medallion quant quantitative statistical arbitrage data science many small edges
Renaissance's Medallion fund, run by mathematicians rather than traders, returned about 66% a year before fees from 1988 to 2018. It trades thousands of instruments over short horizons on many weak statistical patterns, each barely better than a coin flip, combined, sized and executed with great care so that costs don't eat them. Its rules: trust the data, not stories; every signal must be tested out of sample and keep working; never override the model; manage risk across the whole portfolio; and keep the methods secret because published edges decay.
**In Agora:** this is Metis's way of working: many ideas tested, a strict luck check (deflated Sharpe) for the number tried, unseen exam years and a sealed last year, and full charges in every test. Agora's small edges are on daily and intraday prices only, with a much smaller budget for trading costs.

## Ray Dalio: the economic machine and balanced risk
tags: ray dalio bridgewater all weather risk parity debt cycle economic machine principles diversification
Dalio built Bridgewater on a model of the economy as a machine of credit and debt cycles, and on diversification across 15 or more truly uncorrelated return streams, which he called the holy grail of investing because it cuts risk far more than it cuts return. His All Weather portfolio balances risk (not money) across stocks, bonds, gold and commodities so it holds up in growth, recession, inflation and deflation. He writes every decision rule down and back-tests it.
**In Agora:** Agora's portfolio allocator (Phase 15) prefers strategies whose returns don't move together, and the world desk's tilt reads the macro backdrop.

## Ed Thorp: the edge, then the Kelly bet
tags: ed thorp kelly criterion beat the dealer beat the market edge bet sizing convertible arbitrage
Thorp, a mathematician, first beat blackjack by card counting, then the market with warrant and convertible arbitrage, running Princeton Newport at about 20% a year with very low volatility. His two rules: only bet when you have a measurable edge, and size the bet with the Kelly criterion (stake a fraction of capital proportional to edge over odds), in practice using half-Kelly or less because the edge is never known exactly and full Kelly has brutal drawdowns.
**In Agora:** Agora's risk-based sizing (about 1% of equity at risk per trade) is far below Kelly on purpose, since a strategy's edge is uncertain.

## Ed Seykota and Larry Hite: trend following and cutting losses
tags: ed seykota larry hite trend following systems cut losses ride winners whipsaw
Seykota built some of the first computerised trend-following systems in the 1970s and compounded client money spectacularly for decades. His elements of good trading: cut losses, ride winners, keep bets small, follow the rules without question, and know when to break them (rarely). Hite (Mint Investments) said the whole business is about how much you lose when you're wrong: risk at most 1% of capital on a trade, accept many small losses, and catch the big trends that pay for them.
**In Agora:** this is how Kairos's desks trade trends: a stop on every entry, about 1% at risk, a trailing exit and no averaging down.

## Richard Dennis and the Turtles: trading can be taught
tags: richard dennis turtles teach trading rules breakout donchian systematic william eckhardt
Dennis and William Eckhardt argued whether trading could be taught; Dennis trained a group of novices ("Turtles") in 1983–84 on a fully mechanical breakout system: buy 20- or 55-day highs, size by volatility (ATR, "N"), add units as the trade moves your way, and exit on a 10- or 20-day low or a 2N stop. Many Turtles made fortunes, showing that a written edge with strict risk control works even for beginners, provided they follow the rules through losing streaks.
**In Agora:** see "Turtle trading" for the rules; the Donchian breakout family is in Metis's playbook.

## Linda Raschke: short-term swing patterns
tags: linda raschke street smarts short term trading holy grail turtle soup 80-20 swing patterns pullback
Raschke trades short-term swings from a small set of well-tested patterns: buying the first pullback to the 20-day average in a strong ADX trend (the "Holy Grail"), fading failed breakouts of 20-day highs and lows ("Turtle Soup"), and range-expansion and range-contraction setups. She favours trading with the trend after a pause, quick exits, and doing the same few things very well.
**In Agora:** pullback-in-trend and failed-breakout rules are both daily-chart strategies Metis can test; the first pullback after a strong ADX reading is close to the playbook's trend-pullback family.

## Marty Schwartz: from analyst to tape trader
tags: marty schwartz pit bull moving average trend short term index futures discipline cut losses
Schwartz switched from fundamental analysis to technical trading and became a champion trader. His rule: never trade against the 10-day exponential average; only buy above it and sell below it. He focused on risk first, kept losses small, stopped trading after a big loss to regain his footing, and took time off after big wins to avoid giving them back.
**In Agora:** the desk pauses new entries after a 3% losing day, which is Schwartz's "step back after a hit".

## Mark Minervini, William O'Neil, Nicolas Darvas, Stan Weinstein: growth breakouts
tags: growth stock breakout leaders new highs relative strength base stage 2 superperformance
These four share a playbook for growth leaders: buy the strongest stocks (highest relative strength, near 52-week highs) in a rising market, as they break out of a tight base on heavy volume, and sell quickly (7–8% stop) when wrong. Weinstein's stage 2 (price above a rising 30-week average) is the only stage to own; Darvas's boxes and O'Neil's cup-with-handle are base shapes; Minervini's volatility contraction pattern is a base whose pullbacks shrink. They let the big winners run for months. See each method's own section.
**In Agora:** relative strength, nearness to the 52-week high, base breakouts on volume and the 30-week trend are tested families in Metis's playbook.

## Kristjan Kullamägi: momentum bursts in leaders
tags: kristjan kullamagi qullamaggie momentum breakout episodic pivot leaders swing trading
A Swedish swing trader who made large returns from small capital, Kullamägi buys the top momentum stocks (largest gains over one, three and six months) as they break out of a tight, orderly consolidation, risking to the low of the breakout day. He sells part after 3–5 days and trails the rest with the 10- or 20-day average. He also buys "episodic pivots": huge-volume gaps on surprising news. Most of his trades lose a little; a few move 50–100% and make the year.
**In Agora:** a top-momentum universe, a breakout of a narrow range, a partial exit after a few days and a moving-average trail are all testable on daily data; Metis's playbook has the momentum universe and partial exits.

## Peter Brandt: classical chart patterns with strict risk
tags: peter brandt classical charting patterns factor trading futures risk per trade 1 percent
Brandt trades classical chart patterns (rectangles, triangles, head and shoulders, flags) in futures, risking at most about 1% per trade, with fewer than half his trades winning. Most of his profit comes from a few big trends a year. He insists that patterns fail often and that the money comes from risk management and patience, not from the patterns themselves.
**In Agora:** Kairos's pattern detector flags these shapes, and a pattern only becomes a trade with a stop where it's proven wrong.

## Jesse Livermore's rules for money and mind
tags: jesse livermore money management pyramiding sitting tight emotions reminiscences
Beyond his pivotal points, Livermore's lessons were about behaviour: the big money is made by sitting through a trend, not by trading in and out; never average down on a loser; add to a position only when it shows a profit; and the market is never wrong, opinions often are. He went broke several times when he broke his own rules, a lesson in itself.
**In Agora:** adding only to winners and sitting through the trend are the trailing stop and the no-averaging-down rule.

## Rakesh Jhunjhunwala: India's growth, conviction and patience
tags: rakesh jhunjhunwala india big bull titan conviction long term india growth story trading and investing
Jhunjhunwala, "India's Big Bull", started with ₹5,000 in 1985 and built a fortune of billions of dollars. He was both a trader and a long-term investor: he traded actively, but his wealth came from holding a few convictions for decades, Titan above all (bought around 2002–03 and held as it multiplied many times). He bet on India's long growth, chose businesses with large addressable markets and strong management, bought in market pessimism and held through crashes. He said to respect the market, take losses, and keep enough strength to stay in the game.
**In Agora:** his India-growth view is the long-term bull bias behind the Indian daily desk; conviction holds map to wide trailing stops on winners, and trading discipline to the stop on every trade.

## Radhakishan Damani: patience, simplicity, retail
tags: radhakishan damani dmart avenue supermarts value investing india patience consumption
Damani was a successful trader and value investor before founding DMart (Avenue Supermarts). He bought simple, well-run consumer businesses cheaply and held quietly for years; at DMart he applied the same discipline: low costs, owned stores, no debt, slow careful expansion. Mentor to Jhunjhunwala, he is known for patience and saying little.
**In Agora:** his consumption-led India view shows up in the Nifty FMCG and consumption sector indices; his discipline in strategies that trade rarely.

## Vijay Kedia, Ashish Kacholia and Indian small-cap investors
tags: vijay kedia ashish kacholia smiles small cap india multibagger growth investing basant maheshwari
Kedia's SMILE rule (Small in size, Medium in experience, Large in aspiration, Extra-large in market potential) and Kacholia's small and mid-cap picks look for young Indian companies that can grow many times, bought early and held through volatility. They accept a few failures for a few multibaggers, size positions modestly, and avoid companies with poor governance or heavy debt. Basant Maheshwari argues for owning growth even at high valuations when earnings keep compounding.
**In Agora:** small caps move a lot and trade thinly, which is why Kairos's picks need ₹20 crore of daily trading (D-155) and the small-cap index has its own row on the Indices page.

## Raamdeo Agrawal and the QGLP framework
tags: raamdeo agrawal motilal oswal qglp quality growth longevity price wealth creation study india
Agrawal (Motilal Oswal) invests by QGLP: Quality of business and management, Growth in earnings, Longevity of both, and a reasonable Price. His annual wealth-creation studies of Indian companies find that the biggest wealth creators combine high returns on capital, steady earnings growth and a long runway, and that buying them at a fair price and holding them beats trading.
**In Agora:** the quality and momentum factor lists are the price-side shadows of QGLP; holding strong trends longer is its trading form.

## Mark Douglas and Van Tharp: the psychology of consistent trading
tags: mark douglas van tharp trading in the zone psychology probabilistic mindset r multiples position sizing expectancy
Douglas taught that each trade's outcome is random even with an edge, so a trader must think in probabilities over a series of trades, accept risk in advance and execute without hesitation or regret. Tharp taught measuring every trade in R (multiples of the initial risk), judging a system by its expectancy and the spread of R outcomes, and using position sizing as the main lever for reaching goals.
**In Agora:** every trade has a defined risk and Agora reports results in R, expectancy and win rate; desks never skip a signal out of fear.

## Joel Greenblatt: the magic formula
tags: joel greenblatt magic formula return on capital earnings yield value quality special situations
Greenblatt ranks stocks by return on capital (good business) and earnings yield (cheap price), buys the top 20–30 and rebalances yearly. It beat the market over long periods but went through multi-year stretches of underperformance that most investors couldn't sit through, which is why it keeps working. His earlier special-situations investing (spin-offs, restructurings) looked for events that force sellers.
**In Agora:** it needs company accounts, so it isn't backtestable here; its lesson for Metis is that a real edge can lose for years and that the luck check, not one year's result, decides.

## Howard Marks: cycles and second-level thinking
tags: howard marks oaktree market cycles second level thinking risk pendulum credit
Marks (Oaktree) stresses second-level thinking (what does the consensus expect, and what if it's wrong?), knowing where we are in the cycle (the pendulum between greed and fear), and that risk is highest when people feel safest. You can't predict, but you can prepare: be more aggressive when prices are low and fear is high, more defensive when prices are high and complacency rules.
**In Agora:** the regime map and the news risk gauge are the "where are we in the cycle" read; desks stand aside more when the gauge is high.

## John Bogle: costs matter and the index wins
tags: john bogle vanguard index fund costs low cost passive investing buy the haystack
Bogle founded Vanguard and the first retail index fund. His argument: investors as a whole earn the market's return minus costs, so most active managers, after fees and trading costs, trail the index over time. Buy the whole market cheaply and hold.
**In Agora:** this is why every strategy must beat holding the Nifty 50 or the S&P 500 after full charges (D-126): if it can't, the index fund is the better choice.

## How Kairos uses these lessons
tags: kairos desk lessons from legends apply great traders method combination rules
Kairos doesn't copy any one legend; it combines what they agree on with what Agora's own tests show works on these markets. Trend first (Tudor Jones's 200-day line, Weinstein's stage 2). Strength (O'Neil, Minervini, Kullamägi). A stop where the idea is wrong and about 1% at risk (Seykota, Hite, Brandt). Let winners run with a trailing exit (Livermore, Lynch, Jhunjhunwala). Stand aside when the evidence or the backdrop says no (Druckenmiller, Munger, Marks). Many tested, small edges with a strict luck check (Simons, Thorp). And always the index as the bar (Bogle). Where a legend's method needs company accounts (Buffett, Graham, Greenblatt, QGLP), Kairos uses the quality and low-volatility lists as the universe and explains the idea, but its trades come only from tested price rules.
