# How news moves markets: the macro and geopolitics playbook

## How markets usually react to geopolitical shocks
tags: geopolitics war conflict shock market reaction history drawdown recovery lpl
LPL Research studied about two dozen major geopolitical events since 1941 (Pearl Harbor, the Korean war, the Cuban missile crisis, 9/11, the Iraq wars, Crimea, the 2022 invasion of Ukraine and more). The S&P 500's average fall was about 5%, it bottomed after about 18–19 trading days and recovered in about 42 days. The exceptions were long: after Pearl Harbor stocks fell 19.8% and took 307 days to recover. The biggest lesson: what decides the damage is the economy. Shocks that hit during or near a recession (or that cause one, like the 1973 oil embargo) do lasting damage; shocks in a healthy economy are usually sold, then bought back. Selling everything on the first headline has usually locked in losses near the bottom.

## Wars and military conflict
tags: war conflict invasion missile airstrike military escalation ceasefire defence oil
First reaction: stocks fall, oil and gold rise, safe currencies (dollar, yen, Swiss franc) rise, defence stocks rise, airlines and travel fall. Examples: Iraq's invasion of Kuwait (August 1990) doubled oil and took the S&P 500 down about 17% by October, recovering once the air war began in January 1991. On 24 February 2022, the day Russia invaded Ukraine, the Nifty fell about 4.8% and Brent later topped $130. A credible ceasefire or peace talks usually trigger a relief rally (on 12 May 2025, after an India–Pakistan ceasefire, the Nifty jumped about 4%). What to watch: escalation to oil supply (the Strait of Hormuz carries about a fifth of the world's oil), sanctions on major exporters, and whether the big economies are drawn in.

## India–Pakistan tensions and Indian markets
tags: india pakistan kargil uri balakot operation sindoor border tension nifty
Indian markets have mostly looked through past India–Pakistan flare-ups (Kargil 1999, the Uri strikes 2016, Balakot 2019): falls were small and short. On 7 May 2025, the day of Operation Sindoor's strikes, the Nifty closed only 0.33% lower; after the 10 May ceasefire it rallied about 4% in a day. Defence stocks (HAL, BEL, BDL) tend to rise on such news; aviation and tourism dip. The risk to watch is escalation that involves other powers or disrupts trade.

## Terror attacks
tags: terror attack terrorism 9/11 mumbai 26/11 market reaction
After 9/11 the New York Stock Exchange stayed shut for four sessions; when it reopened the S&P 500 fell about 12% in a week, then regained its pre-attack level within about a month. Indian markets reopened quickly after the 2008 Mumbai attacks with limited damage. Markets react to terror mainly through what follows (wars, confidence, travel), not the event itself.

## Sanctions and trade wars (tariffs)
tags: sanctions tariffs trade war liberation day us china exports supply chains
Sanctions on a big exporter (Russia 2022) lift that commodity's price (oil, gas, wheat, metals) and hurt importers. Tariffs hit exporters and companies with foreign supply chains, raise inflation, and can slow growth. The US–China trade war (2018–2019) caused several 5–20% corrections. On 2 April 2025 ("Liberation Day") broad US tariffs sent the S&P 500 down about 12% in four sessions; a 90-day pause on 9 April gave one of its best days ever (+9.5%), and after a US–China truce on 12 May it erased the year's losses. For India: US tariffs on Indian goods hurt IT services sentiment, textiles, auto parts, gems and seafood exporters. Tariff news is often reversed or delayed: wait for details.

## Central banks: the Fed, the RBI, the ECB
tags: central bank fed fomc rbi mpc ecb interest rates rate hike rate cut hawkish dovish quantitative tightening
Interest rates are the price of money: higher rates lower the value of future profits (growth and tech stocks suffer most), raise borrowing costs (real estate, autos, small companies) and strengthen the currency. Rate cuts help, unless they are cutting because a recession is coming (2001 and 2007 cuts came before bear markets). Markets move on the surprise versus expectations and on the guidance ("hawkish" = more hikes, "dovish" = cuts). The Fed meets eight times a year; the RBI's monetary policy committee six times. In 2022 the fastest Fed hikes in decades took the S&P 500 down about 25% and the Nasdaq about 35%.

## Inflation, jobs and growth data
tags: inflation cpi wpi jobs payrolls unemployment gdp pmi recession data release
US CPI (monthly, mid-month) and the jobs report (first Friday) move global markets because they drive the Fed. Hotter-than-expected inflation is bad for stocks and bonds; strong jobs can be good (growth) or bad (more hikes) depending on the moment. India's CPI (around the 12th), GDP (quarterly) and PMI matter for the RBI. Purchasing-manager indices (PMI) below 50 mean contraction. A recession is the single biggest driver of deep bear markets.

## Oil, energy and commodities
tags: oil crude brent opec energy prices commodity shock india importer
India imports about 85% of its oil, so higher oil means a weaker rupee, higher inflation and a wider deficit: bad for Indian stocks overall. Losers: oil marketing companies (BPCL, HPCL, IOC), airlines (IndiGo), paints (Asian Paints), tyres and chemicals. Winners: producers (ONGC, Oil India) and, in the US, energy stocks. OPEC+ output decisions, Middle East supply risks and sanctions are the main oil news. The 1973–74 oil shock, with a recession, took US stocks down about 48%.

## Banking crises and contagion
tags: banking crisis bank run contagion lehman svb credit suisse liquidity bailout
Banking stress spreads fast because banks lend to each other. 2008: after Lehman Brothers failed (15 September), global markets crashed; the S&P 500 fell about 57% from its 2007 peak and the Sensex about 60% from its January 2008 high. March 2023: Silicon Valley Bank's failure caused a bank-stock crash and the rescue of Credit Suisse, but quick central-bank action limited the damage. Signs: deposit flight, credit spreads widening, bank shares falling far more than the market. In a banking scare, banks and financials fall first and most.

## Government debt, ratings and bond markets
tags: sovereign debt credit rating downgrade bond yields debt ceiling government shutdown yield curve
When bond yields jump, stocks usually fall: future profits are worth less and bonds compete with shares. S&P's downgrade of the US in August 2011 was followed by a 6.7% fall in the S&P 500 on the next trading day. US debt-ceiling standoffs and shutdowns cause short scares. An inverted yield curve (short rates above long rates) has come before most US recessions, usually by 6–24 months.

## Currencies: the rupee and the dollar
tags: currency rupee dollar index fx reserves depreciation fii outflows taper tantrum
A falling rupee helps exporters (IT services like TCS and Infosys, pharma) and hurts importers and companies with dollar debt; it usually comes with foreign investors (FIIs) selling Indian shares. In the 2013 "taper tantrum" the rupee hit a record low near 68.8 per dollar in August. A strong US dollar generally pressures emerging markets and commodities. The RBI uses its reserves to smooth sharp moves.

## Elections and political shocks
tags: election results exit polls political uncertainty coup government collapse india 2024
Markets dislike uncertainty more than any party. On 4 June 2024, when India's election results fell far short of exit-poll expectations, the Nifty dropped about 5.9% (8.5% at the worst point of the day), its biggest fall in four years; it recovered within days to new highs once a coalition government was clear. Coups, snap elections and impeachments in major economies cause short sell-offs; policy continuity brings relief rallies.

## Pandemics and health emergencies
tags: pandemic covid lockdown outbreak health emergency crash march 2020
COVID-19: from 19 February to 23 March 2020 the S&P 500 fell about 34%; the Nifty fell about 38% between January and late March, with market-wide circuit breakers hit on 13 and 23 March (the Nifty fell 13% on 23 March). Massive stimulus then drove one of the fastest recoveries ever: the S&P 500 regained its high by August 2020, the Nifty by late 2020. Outbreaks that stay local (Ebola, bird flu) usually have little market effect.

## Natural disasters and cyber attacks
tags: earthquake cyclone flood monsoon disaster cyber attack outage
Disasters move markets when they hit big economies or supply chains: Japan's 2011 earthquake and tsunami sent the Nikkei down about 16% in two days. In India, a weak monsoon hurts rural demand (FMCG, two-wheelers, tractors, fertilisers); a normal one helps. Big cyber attacks and IT outages hit the companies involved and briefly whole sectors.

## Company news: what really moves a stock
tags: company news earnings guidance downgrade fraud probe ceo resigns promoter pledge block deal index inclusion
Quarterly results against expectations (and guidance for the next quarters) move stocks most; a good result that was already expected can still fall. Red flags that often lead to big, lasting falls: fraud or accounting questions (Satyam fell about 78% in one day in January 2009 after its chairman's confession), regulator raids and probes, auditor resignations, the CEO or CFO quitting suddenly, defaults and promoter share pledges being invoked. A short seller's report (Hindenburg on the Adani group, January 2023, wiped out over $100 billion in weeks) can be devastating. Positive catalysts: big orders, upgrades, buybacks, index inclusion.

## Priced in: reading news like a professional
tags: priced in surprise expectations buy the rumour sell the news reaction
What moves prices is the surprise, not the news. "Buy the rumour, sell the news": expected good news is often sold. Watch the reaction: a market that rises on bad news is strong; one that falls on good news is weak. The first move after a shock is often the most emotional; the trend after the first day tells you more. One headline from one source is weak evidence; the same story from many reputable sources, with prices confirming, is strong. That is exactly how Kairos's news risk gauge and news guard weigh the news.

## Sectors that usually win and lose on big news (India and US)
tags: sectors winners losers defence energy airlines it exporters banks real estate gold safe haven
War or Middle East tension: defence, oil producers and gold up; airlines, paints, oil marketing companies and travel down. Rising rates: banks mixed, real estate, autos, small caps and growth/tech down. Falling rupee: IT services and pharma up. US tariffs on India: exporters (IT services sentiment, textiles, auto parts, gems, seafood) down. Recession fears: defensives (FMCG, pharma, utilities) hold up better than cyclicals (metals, autos, capital goods). Risk-off days: large, liquid stocks fall less than small caps.
