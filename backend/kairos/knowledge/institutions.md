# Institutional investors and how big money moves markets

## Who the institutions are
tags: institutional investors fii fpi dii mutual funds insurance pension hedge funds smart money
Institutions are the big players whose orders move prices: foreign portfolio investors (FPIs, still often called FIIs), domestic institutions (DIIs: mutual funds, LIC and other insurers, pension funds such as EPFO and NPS), hedge funds, proprietary trading desks and market makers. In India FPIs own about a sixth to a fifth of listed shares and DIIs now a similar share; retail investors own less directly but trade a large part of the daily volume. Institutions decide the trend of large stocks over weeks; retail decides much of the noise inside a day.

## FII and DII flows in India
tags: fii dii flows foreign selling domestic buying sip nse provisional data
NSE publishes provisional FII/FPI and DII cash-market buying and selling every evening. Heavy, persistent FPI selling (often when the US dollar strengthens, US yields rise, or other markets look cheaper) weighs on large caps, banks and IT; DIIs, fed by monthly SIP inflows of mutual funds (over ₹20,000 crore a month since 2024), have often absorbed it, which is why Indian dips have been bought faster than before. One day's flow means little; a run of weeks in one direction is a regime. Use it as context for the market's trend, not as a trigger.

## Why the rupee, the dollar and US yields matter
tags: rupee dollar dxy us yields bond yields foreign flows emerging markets
FPIs measure returns in dollars. A falling rupee cuts their returns, and higher US Treasury yields make safe dollar assets more attractive, so both tend to bring FPI selling in Indian shares, especially banks and large caps. A softer dollar and falling US yields usually bring them back. Agora's world desk tracks these.

## How institutions buy: slowly, and around VWAP
tags: institutional buying accumulation vwap twap execution algorithms footprints volume
A fund that wants ₹500 crore of a stock can't buy it in one order; its execution algorithms split it over days or weeks, often aiming at the day's VWAP. That leaves footprints: rising prices on rising volume, shallow pullbacks on light volume, closes near the day's high, a stock holding up while the index falls (relative strength), and a high delivery percentage on NSE (shares actually taken, not just traded intraday). These are the signs of accumulation; the reverse (heavy volume on down days, weak closes) is distribution.

## Delivery percentage (India)
tags: delivery percentage delivery volume nse accumulation intraday speculation
NSE reports how much of each day's volume ended in delivery. A price rise with a delivery share well above the stock's usual (say 60% against a normal 35%) suggests real buyers taking shares home, often institutions; a rise on low delivery is mostly intraday speculation and fades more easily.

## Index rebalancing (MSCI, FTSE, Nifty)
tags: index rebalancing msci ftse nifty inclusion exclusion passive funds weights
Passive funds must buy stocks added to an index and sell those removed, on the effective date's close, whatever the price. MSCI reviews in February, May, August and November; FTSE and Nifty indexes have their own schedules. Stocks often run up between the announcement and the effective date and fade after; huge volumes print in the closing minutes of that day. Weight increases (more of a company's shares counted as free float) work the same way.

## Bulk and block deals, promoters and pledges
tags: bulk deal block deal promoter buying selling pledge insider stake sale ofs
NSE publishes bulk deals (over 0.5% of a company's shares in a day) and block deals (large trades in the special morning window). A known fund buying a big block is a vote of confidence; a promoter or private-equity investor selling a large stake (an OFS or a block) often caps the price for a while. Rising promoter pledges are a warning: if the price falls, lenders sell the pledged shares, which pushes it down further.

## Derivatives positioning: open interest, PCR and expiry
tags: open interest oi put call ratio pcr max pain expiry option writers fno positioning
In India's very active options market, institutions and big traders are mostly option sellers. Heavy call open interest at a strike tends to act as resistance and heavy put open interest as support, especially into the weekly expiry; the strike where option buyers lose most ("max pain") often pulls the index near it on expiry day. The put-call ratio of open interest above about 1.2–1.3 means many puts sold (traders confident of support), below about 0.7 the opposite; extremes often precede reversals. Rising price with rising open interest means new longs; rising price with falling open interest means shorts covering, which fades sooner.

## Quarter-end, results season and window dressing
tags: quarter end window dressing results season earnings march fiscal year
Funds tidy their portfolios before quarter-end reports ("window dressing"): winners get bought and losers sold in the last days of March, June, September and December. Results season (mid-January, April, July, October in India) brings the biggest single-stock moves of the year; institutions reposition around the numbers and the management's guidance, not the headline.

## Stop hunts and liquidity: why obvious levels get broken
tags: stop hunt liquidity grab false breakout support resistance trap retail stops
Large orders need someone on the other side. Obvious levels (round numbers, yesterday's low, a widely seen support) collect many retail stop-losses, and a quick push through them gives big buyers the shares they want at good prices before the price snaps back. That is why a stop placed exactly at the obvious level gets hit so often. Put stops a little beyond the level and at least about one ATR away, size by the stop, and wait for a close back above the level before buying a failed breakdown.

## US institutions: 13F filings, options expiry and the Fed
tags: 13f hedge funds us institutions opex gamma fed fomc buybacks
In the US, funds over $100 million disclose their holdings each quarter (13F filings, 45 days late, so they are history, not signals). Monthly options expiry (the third Friday) and market makers' hedging (gamma) can pin prices near big strikes before expiry and speed up moves after. Company buybacks are a steady source of buying, paused in the weeks before results. FOMC decisions, CPI and jobs reports move the whole market, and India often follows the next morning.

## Trading alongside institutions as a retail trader
tags: retail edge follow institutions small size nimble trade with the trend
A retail trader can't move prices, but can move fast and needs no permission. The edge is to trade **with** institutional flow, not against it: buy stocks showing accumulation (relative strength, rising volume, high delivery) in sectors that are receiving money, in a market above its 200-day average; avoid stocks under steady distribution; and stand aside, or trade small, when FPIs sell heavily and the rupee is weak.
