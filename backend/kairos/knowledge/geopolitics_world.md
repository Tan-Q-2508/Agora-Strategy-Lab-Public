# The world desk: geopolitics, how events travel, and how to read a sudden move

## How a far-away event reaches a stock price
tags: geopolitics transmission channels contagion oil currency rates supply chain risk appetite flows second-order
An event in one corner of the world reaches markets through a few channels, usually in this order: (1) **risk appetite**: big funds sell risky assets everywhere within minutes (stock futures, emerging markets, small caps first) and buy safety (the dollar, the yen, the Swiss franc, gold, US Treasuries); (2) **commodities**: wars, sanctions and storms near producers move oil, gas, wheat, fertilisers and metals; (3) **currencies and rates**: a dearer dollar and higher yields pull money out of emerging markets such as India; (4) **supply chains and trade**: blocked routes, tariffs and export bans raise costs and cut sales for specific companies; (5) **confidence**: consumers and companies delay spending. A good analyst asks of every headline: which channel, how big, who is exposed, and is it already priced in? The first move is often the biggest; the lasting move depends on whether the economy itself is hurt.

## Chokepoints: the narrow places the world's trade passes through
tags: chokepoint strait of hormuz red sea bab el-mandeb suez malacca bosporus black sea panama taiwan strait shipping
**Strait of Hormuz** (Iran/Oman): about a fifth of the world's oil and a large share of LNG (Qatar's) pass through it; a closure is the classic worst case for oil. **Bab el-Mandeb and the Red Sea to Suez**: roughly a tenth of world trade and much of Europe–Asia container traffic; Houthi attacks from late 2023 pushed ships around Africa, adding 10–14 days and lifting freight rates several-fold. **Strait of Malacca**: the shortest route between the Indian Ocean and the Pacific; most of the oil China, Japan and Korea import. **Bosporus** (Turkey): Black Sea grain and Russian oil. **Panama Canal**: a 2023 drought cut transits. **Taiwan Strait**: a busy shipping lane next to the world's most advanced chip factories. When a headline names one of these, think oil, freight, delivery delays and insurance first.

## Who exports what: the commodity map
tags: commodities exporters russia ukraine saudi qatar australia chile china rare earths taiwan chips wheat fertiliser palladium nickel lng copper
**Oil**: the US (the largest producer), Saudi Arabia, Russia, Canada, Iraq, the UAE, Iran, Kuwait. **Gas/LNG**: the US, Qatar, Australia, Russia (pipelines to Europe and China). **Wheat**: Russia (the largest exporter), the EU, Canada, Australia, the US, Ukraine. **Sunflower oil**: Ukraine and Russia, together most of world exports. **Fertilisers**: Russia, Belarus (potash), China, Morocco (phosphate), the Gulf (urea from gas). **Metals**: Chile and Peru (copper), Australia and Brazil (iron ore), Russia (palladium, nickel, aluminium), Indonesia (nickel), the DRC (cobalt). **Rare earths**: China mines most and processes about 90%; its export curbs hit chip, magnet, EV and defence makers. **Chips**: Taiwan (the most advanced logic, TSMC), Korea (memory), the Netherlands (ASML's lithography machines), the US (design and equipment), Japan (materials). **Drugs**: India (generics), China (drug ingredients, APIs). An event near any of these lifts that commodity's price and hurts its buyers.

## India's weak spots and strengths in world events
tags: india vulnerability oil import rupee remittances gulf fii flows it exports us tariffs monsoon gold edible oil china api electronics
India imports about 85% of its crude oil (and most of its gas, edible oil and gold), so dear oil widens the trade deficit, weakens the rupee and lifts inflation, which can stop the RBI from cutting. Foreign portfolio investors (FPIs/FIIs) own a large share of Indian shares and sell in global risk-off moments; the rupee and the Nifty often fall together. India is the world's largest recipient of remittances, a big share from workers in the Gulf, so a Gulf war hurts households too. Strengths: services exports (IT, earning in dollars, mostly from the US and Europe), generic drugs, a large domestic market that cushions world slowdowns, and a "China plus one" pull for factories. Weak spots in trade: drug ingredients and electronics parts from China, and US tariffs on textiles, gems, seafood and auto parts. The monsoon still drives rural demand.

## America's weak spots and strengths in world events
tags: united states vulnerability china supply chain taiwan chips dollar treasury energy producer consumer inflation
The US is now the world's largest oil and gas producer, so dear oil hurts US consumers and airlines but helps its energy companies and the trade balance; the overall hit is smaller than in the 1970s. Its exposure is to supply chains in China and chips from Taiwan (Apple, Nvidia, AMD, Qualcomm, carmakers), to tariffs it imposes itself (higher prices, retaliation), and to its own fiscal fights (shutdowns, the debt ceiling). The dollar and Treasuries are the world's safe havens, so world crises usually lift the dollar and lower yields unless the crisis is about the US itself. Defence makers (Lockheed Martin, RTX, Northrop Grumman, General Dynamics) gain from wars and higher NATO spending.

## Oil shocks: what history says
tags: oil shock 1973 embargo 1979 iran revolution 1990 kuwait 2008 spike 2022 ukraine opec supply recession
**1973–74** (Arab oil embargo): oil roughly quadrupled; with a recession, US stocks fell about 48% peak to trough. **1979** (Iranian revolution): oil more than doubled; inflation and the Fed's high rates hurt stocks. **1990** (Iraq invades Kuwait): oil about doubled in two months; the S&P 500 fell about 20% into October and recovered once the air war began in January 1991. **2008**: Brent near $147 in July, then a crash with the financial crisis. **2022** (Russia–Ukraine): Brent above $120; inflation and rate hikes followed. The pattern: oil spikes hurt most when they cause or meet a recession; quick spikes that fade (2019 Abqaiq attack, oil up about 15% in a day and back within weeks) do little lasting harm. India's market is more sensitive than America's to oil.

## The Middle East: who is who and what matters to markets
tags: middle east israel iran hezbollah hamas houthis saudi arabia gulf proxy war oil hormuz ceasefire escalation ladder
What matters to markets is oil flows and escalation between states, not each strike. The escalation ladder markets watch: proxy fighting (Hamas, Hezbollah, the Houthis) → direct Israel–Iran exchanges (April and October 2024, June 2025) → attacks on oil facilities or shipping (Abqaiq 2019, the Red Sea from 2023) → a threat to the Strait of Hormuz → US involvement. Each rung lifts oil and volatility more. Markets have usually sold the first headline and recovered within weeks when oil supply was not actually cut: in June 2025, after US strikes on Iran's nuclear sites, oil fell once Iran's response was limited and a ceasefire followed. Saudi Arabia and the UAE are the swing producers with spare capacity; their stance calms or inflames oil.

## Russia, Ukraine and NATO
tags: russia ukraine nato sanctions price cap europe energy crisis gas wheat fertiliser defence spending
Russia's invasion of Ukraine (24 February 2022) lifted oil, gas, wheat, fertiliser and metal prices, caused Europe's energy crisis, and started the biggest rise in NATO defence spending since the Cold War. Sanctions, the G7 price cap on Russian oil and the EU's ban on Russian seaborne crude rerouted oil to India, China and Turkey at a discount. For India this helped refiners and the import bill but created a political risk: in August 2025 the US added a 25% tariff on Indian goods over Russian oil purchases. Watch: peace talks (oil and gas down, European stocks up, defence down), escalation with NATO (risk-off), attacks on energy infrastructure and Black Sea shipping (wheat, oil).

## China and Taiwan: the chip risk
tags: china taiwan tsmc semiconductors blockade drills pelosi export controls nvidia apple supply chain
TSMC in Taiwan makes about 90% of the most advanced chips, used by Apple, Nvidia, AMD, Qualcomm and most AI servers. Chinese military drills around Taiwan (after Nancy Pelosi's August 2022 visit, and since) briefly hit chip stocks and lift volatility; a blockade or war would be the largest supply shock in decades, hitting tech hardware, autos and every industry that uses chips. Signals to watch: PLA drill size and duration, US arms sales, statements around Taiwan's elections, shipping and insurance notices. Markets usually treat drills as noise unless they disrupt shipping.

## The US–China rivalry: tariffs, export controls, rare earths
tags: us china trade war tariffs 2018 2019 export controls entity list rare earth geneva truce liberation day
The 2018–2019 trade war came in rounds: each tariff escalation (March 2018, May 2019, August 2019) brought 5–20% corrections in US stocks, each truce a rally. From October 2022 the US curbed exports of advanced chips and chip tools to China; China answered with curbs on gallium, germanium, graphite and rare earths. On 2 April 2025 broad US tariffs hit almost every country; after escalation with China to triple-digit rates, a truce agreed in Geneva on 12 May 2025 cut them sharply and markets rallied. Who suffers: US companies selling in or making in China, US farmers (soybeans), chip-equipment makers. Who can gain: India, Vietnam and Mexico as factories move ("China plus one"), local chip makers.

## India and its neighbours: Pakistan and China
tags: india pakistan kashmir terror surgical strike balakot operation sindoor ceasefire india china galwan border defence
Indian markets have mostly looked through India–Pakistan flare-ups: small, short dips (Kargil 1999, the Uri surgical strikes in September 2016 with the Nifty about 1.8% lower that day, Balakot 2019, Operation Sindoor in May 2025, after which a ceasefire brought a 4% rally). Defence stocks (HAL, BEL, BDL, Mazagon Dock, Bharat Dynamics) usually rise; airlines and tourism dip. The risk to watch is escalation involving other powers. With China, the June 2020 Galwan clash led to app bans, curbs on Chinese investment and a push for local manufacturing; trade dependence on Chinese drug ingredients and electronics parts is the exposure.

## Sanctions: how they work and who pays
tags: sanctions secondary sanctions swift price cap ofac asset freeze export ban iran venezuela russia
Primary sanctions stop a country's own companies dealing with a target; **secondary sanctions** punish third countries that keep dealing with it, which is what moves markets in India or China. Common tools: asset freezes, cutting banks off SWIFT, export bans (chips), import bans (oil), and price caps. Sanctions on a big commodity exporter (Iran, Venezuela, Russia) tighten supply and lift that commodity; sanctions relief (the 2015 Iran deal) does the opposite. Companies with business in the target country fall; competitors and alternative suppliers gain.

## Central banks carry geopolitics into every market
tags: fed dollar emerging markets taper tantrum rbi intervention rupee capital flows rate differential
When the Fed raises rates (or surprises hawkishly), the dollar rises and money leaves emerging markets: the May 2013 "taper tantrum" pushed the rupee to a record low near 68.8 per dollar by August and hit Indian banks and rate-sensitive stocks. A Fed cut usually brings money back. The RBI uses its reserves to smooth the rupee and may hold or hike rates to defend it, which hurts Indian lenders and property. War-driven oil spikes can also keep central banks from cutting. So a geopolitical shock often reaches Indian banks through the RBI, not directly.

## Currency crises and contagion between countries
tags: currency crisis contagion asian financial crisis russia default turkey fragile five capital flight
Crises spread from one country to others that look similar to investors: the 1997 Asian crisis started with Thailand's baht and spread across Asia; Russia's 1998 default sank the hedge fund LTCM; the 2013 "fragile five" (India, Indonesia, Brazil, Turkey, South Africa) were hit together because they relied on foreign money. Signs: a currency falling fast, foreign-exchange reserves dropping, bond yields jumping, foreign investors selling. India today holds large reserves, which makes such contagion milder than in 2013.

## Elections and politics as market events
tags: election exit poll result uncertainty policy continuity coalition shutdown debt ceiling impeachment coup
Markets fear uncertainty, not parties. India: a clear mandate (May 2014, May 2019) brought rallies; a surprise (4 June 2024, about 6% down in a day) a sharp fall and quick recovery once a government was clear. Government-owned companies (PSU banks, defence, railways, power) move most on policy continuity. US: shutdowns and debt-ceiling fights cause short scares (August 2011 downgrade: about 6.7% down the next day); presidential results move sectors (energy, banks, healthcare, clean energy) more than the index. Coups and snap elections in big economies cause brief sell-offs.

## Pandemics, disasters and the weather
tags: pandemic covid lockdown earthquake tsunami fukushima hurricane el nino monsoon drought flood insurance
COVID-19: the S&P 500 fell about 34% (February–March 2020) and the Nifty about 38%; massive stimulus drove one of the fastest recoveries ever. Travel, airlines and hotels fell most; drug makers, online platforms and staples held up; a vaccine announcement (9 November 2020) sparked a rotation into travel and banks. Japan's 2011 earthquake: the Nikkei fell about 16% in two days. In India, El Niño years tend to bring weaker monsoons, which hurt rural demand (tractors, two-wheelers, fertilisers, FMCG) and lift food prices.

## India: which sectors gain and lose from each kind of news
tags: india sector map oil omc paints tyres airlines rupee it pharma metals china rates banks realty defence rural
Dear oil hurts fuel retailers (BPCL, HPCL, IOC), airlines (IndiGo), paints (Asian Paints, Berger), tyres (MRF, Apollo, CEAT) and chemicals; it helps ONGC and Oil India. A weak rupee helps IT services (TCS, Infosys, HCLTech) and pharma exporters; it hurts importers. Rate cuts help banks, lenders (Bajaj Finance), property (DLF, Godrej Properties) and autos. China stimulus helps metals (Tata Steel, JSW Steel, Hindalco, Vedanta). US tariffs on India hurt textiles, gems, seafood, auto parts and IT sentiment. Wars with neighbours help defence (HAL, BEL). A good monsoon helps tractors (M&M), two-wheelers (Hero), fertilisers and FMCG. Government capex helps capital goods (L&T, Siemens, ABB), cement (UltraTech) and railways.

## The US: which sectors gain and lose from each kind of news
tags: us sector map energy defence airlines semiconductors banks regional banks homebuilders utilities staples rates dollar
Dear oil: energy (ExxonMobil, Chevron, ConocoPhillips) up, airlines (Delta, United) and cruise lines down. Wars and NATO spending: defence (Lockheed Martin, RTX, Northrop Grumman, General Dynamics) up. Taiwan and export controls: chips (Nvidia, AMD, Broadcom, Applied Materials, Lam Research) down. Rate cuts: homebuilders, REITs, regional banks and small caps up; hikes the reverse. Recession fears: staples (Procter & Gamble, Coca-Cola), utilities and health care hold up; banks, industrials and materials fall. A stronger dollar hurts multinationals' foreign earnings. Banking scares: regional banks (the KRE fund) fall most.

## Safe havens and the order things move in
tags: safe haven risk off risk on gold dollar yen swiss franc treasuries vix order of moves futures
In a risk-off shock the order is usually: stock futures and currencies (minutes) → gold, the yen, the Swiss franc and Treasuries rise → volatility (VIX, India VIX) jumps → stocks fall, emerging markets and small caps most → credit spreads widen if it lasts. The reverse happens on relief. So when Indian stocks fall at the open, check what happened overnight: US futures, Asian markets, Brent crude, the dollar index, US 10-year yields and USD/INR. If oil and gold jumped and the rupee fell, a geopolitical shock is likely; if only stocks fell, look for flows, earnings or local news.

## How long shocks last, and "priced in"
tags: priced in buy the rumour sell the news recovery time drawdown average geopolitical shock lpl history
Across about two dozen major geopolitical events since 1941, the S&P 500 fell about 5% on average, bottomed after about three weeks and recovered in about six (LPL Research). The exceptions came with recessions. Markets move on surprises: an expected rate cut or a long-rumoured tariff often moves little on the day ("buy the rumour, sell the news"). A shock already on every front page for weeks is mostly priced in; the market then reacts to escalation or relief. Selling on the first headline has usually meant selling near the low.

## Second- and third-order effects: thinking one step further
tags: second order effects third order beneficiaries china plus one europe gas us lng defence steel freight inflation
Good analysts follow the chain past the first step. War → defence orders → steel, aluminium, electronics suppliers. Red Sea attacks → freight up → goods inflation → central banks wait to cut → rate-sensitive stocks lag. Europe's gas crisis (2022) → US LNG exporters gain. Sanctions on Russian oil → discounted crude for Indian refiners. US–China decoupling → factories move to India, Vietnam, Mexico → Indian electronics makers (Dixon, Kaynes) and industrial parks gain. A strong dollar → emerging-market outflows → Indian banks. Ask "who supplies the winners?" and "who buys from the losers?"

## Why did the market suddenly fall? A checklist
tags: sudden fall why did the market fall crash sell-off investigation checklist cause attribution overnight expiry fii flows
When the Nifty or a sector drops suddenly, check in order: (1) **overnight and global**: did the US, Asia or Europe fall first? (contagion); (2) **world news** in the last 18 hours: wars, tariffs, sanctions, oil, the Fed; (3) **cross-asset clues**: crude, gold, the dollar, USD/INR, US yields, VIX/India VIX: a geopolitical shock moves several of them together; (4) **domestic news**: RBI, SEBI rules, tax changes, election news; (5) **sector or company news**: one heavyweight's results or a regulatory action can drag a sector index; (6) **flows and mechanics**: FII selling, F&O expiry, index rebalancing, block deals, margin calls; (7) **technical**: a key level breaking can trigger stop-loss selling with no news at all. Agora's move investigator runs this list in code and says how sure it is.

## Moves without news: expiry, rebalancing, flows and stops
tags: no news move expiry rebalancing msci block deal margin call stop loss algorithmic selling circuit breaker liquidity
Many sharp moves have no single news cause. F&O expiry days bring large hedging flows. Index rebalancing (MSCI, FTSE, Nifty reviews) forces funds to buy or sell named stocks on a set day. Block deals (a promoter or fund selling a big stake) hit one stock and sometimes its sector. Margin calls and leveraged positions unwinding (the yen carry trade, 5 August 2024) cause falls that feed on themselves. When a well-watched level breaks, stop-losses and algorithms sell together. Circuit breakers halt trading after limit moves. If the investigator finds no news, these are the usual suspects.

## Sector rotation and the business cycle
tags: sector rotation business cycle early cycle late cycle recession defensives cyclicals relative strength rrg
Sectors take turns leading as the economy moves through its cycle: early recovery (financials, consumer discretionary, real estate, small caps), mid cycle (technology, industrials, communication services), late cycle (energy, materials, as commodities tighten), and recession (consumer staples, utilities, health care). Money rotating between sectors shows up as relative strength: a sector beating the index with rising momentum is "leading", one beating but slowing is "weakening", one trailing but improving is "improving" (Relative Rotation Graphs). Leadership usually lasts months, not days, which is why sector strength is a useful filter for stock picks.

## When a whole sector booms
tags: sector boom bloom rally breadth participation catalyst sympathy move chase leaders laggards policy pli capex
A sector "boom" is a rising tide: most of its stocks rise together, beyond the market, on more volume than usual, usually on a catalyst: a policy (production-linked incentives, defence indigenisation, railway or power capex), a commodity cycle (metals with China stimulus), a rate cycle (banks and property with cuts) or a technology wave (AI chips and power). Healthy booms have broad participation (most members above their 50-day average) and leaders making new highs. Late booms show narrowing breadth, the weakest stocks rallying hardest ("sympathy moves") and huge volume spikes. Buying the leaders on pullbacks has historically worked better than chasing the laggards.

## Multiple timeframe analysis (top-down, Elder's triple screen)
tags: multiple timeframe analysis mtf top down triple screen elder weekly daily hourly confirmation false signal trend filter
Traders read the same stock on several timeframes: the bigger chart sets the direction, the chart you trade on finds the setup, a smaller one times the entry (Alexander Elder's "triple screen", 1985). A buy signal on the daily chart while the weekly chart is in a downtrend is a "bounce in a downtrend" and fails more often; a dip on the daily chart inside a weekly uptrend is a pullback trend followers buy. Requiring the bigger chart to agree cuts trades but removes many false alarms (a common rule: only trade long when the weekly close is above its 30- or 40-week average). Agora reads the monthly, weekly and daily charts together, strategies can use any condition "on the weekly chart", and the Kairos desk only trades with the bigger chart's trend.
