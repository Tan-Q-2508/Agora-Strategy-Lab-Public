# More chart patterns, and what the statistics say

## What the pattern statistics say (Bulkowski)
tags: bulkowski statistics chart pattern performance failure rate encyclopedia of chart patterns
Thomas Bulkowski's Encyclopedia of Chart Patterns measured thousands of patterns on US stocks. Best performers by average move after the breakout (bull markets, "perfect trades" without costs): bump-and-run reversal bottom (+55%), cup with handle (+54%), rectangle top (+51%), double bottom Eve & Eve (+50%), rounding bottom (+47–48%), head-and-shoulders bottom complex (+47%), diamond bottom (+47%), ascending triangle (+43%). Rising and falling wedges and symmetrical triangles ranked among the weakest. His failure-rate study (1991–2008) found chart patterns fail two to four times more often now than in the 1990s: in 2007 about 44% of upward breakouts failed to rise even 10%. Lessons: patterns give an edge, not certainty; stops are essential; and Kairos measures each pattern on your own market's history instead of trusting textbook numbers.

## Harmonic patterns: the idea
tags: harmonic patterns xabcd fibonacci potential reversal zone prz carney gartley pesavento
Harmonic patterns are five-point zig-zags (X, A, B, C, D) whose legs stand in Fibonacci ratios. H.M. Gartley described the first in 1935; Larry Pesavento, Bryce Gilmore and Scott Carney defined the modern ratio sets. The pattern completes at D, the "potential reversal zone"; traders buy (or sell) there with a stop just beyond, and take profits at the 38.2% and 61.8% retracements of A to D. Kairos needs a close back away from D (1 ATR or 23.6% of CD) to confirm, and treats a close 1 ATR beyond D as a failure. Harmonics are precise but subjective in where swings start; their measured record in academic tests is mixed, so position size matters more than the exact ratio.

## Gartley
tags: gartley gartley_bull gartley_bear 222 harmonic
The Gartley ("222" pattern): B retraces 61.8% of XA, C retraces 38.2–88.6% of AB, and D completes at 78.6% of XA, with CD 1.272–1.618× BC. D stays inside X, so the stop is just beyond X. The original and the most traded harmonic.

## Bat and alternate bat
tags: bat bat_bull bat_bear alt_bat alt_bat_bull alt_bat_bear alternate bat harmonic carney
Scott Carney's bat (2001): B retraces only 38.2–50% of XA, D completes deep at 88.6% of XA, with CD 1.618–2.618× BC. The alternate bat has a shallower B (at most 38.2%) and D overshooting X to 113% of XA, with CD 2.0–3.618× BC.

## Butterfly, crab and deep crab
tags: butterfly butterfly_bull butterfly_bear crab crab_bull crab_bear deep_crab deep_crab_bull deep_crab_bear extension harmonic
These complete beyond X (extension patterns), at extreme prices. Butterfly (Bryce Gilmore): B at 78.6% of XA, D at 127–161.8% of XA, CD 1.618–2.618× BC. Crab (Carney, 2000): B at 38.2–61.8%, D at 161.8% of XA, CD 2.24–3.618× BC, the most extended. Deep crab: B at 88.6%, D at 161.8%. Because D is far beyond the start, reward can be large but the move into D is often violent; wait for the confirmation.

## Cypher and shark
tags: cypher cypher_bull cypher_bear shark shark_bull shark_bear oglesbee harmonic
In both, C overshoots A. Cypher (Darren Oglesbee): B at 38.2–61.8% of XA, C extends to 127.2–141.4% of XA (measured from X), and D retraces 78.6% of X-to-C. Shark (Carney, 2011): C extends 113–161.8% of AB beyond A, and D comes back 88.6–113% of X-to-C, with CD 1.618–2.24× BC. Both are newer and less tested than the Gartley.

## AB=CD and three drives
tags: abcd abcd_bull abcd_bear three_drives three_drives_bull three_drives_bear measured move symmetry
AB=CD: two equal legs (CD equal to AB, or extended 1.272× or 1.618×) separated by a 38.2–88.6% pullback; the reversal is expected at D. It is the building block of every harmonic and close to the classic "measured move". Three drives: three pushes in one direction, each a 1.27–1.618 extension of the pullback before it, pullbacks retracing 61.8–78.6%: buyers (or sellers) exhausting themselves. Often the same shape as a wedge, where Kairos prefers the wedge name.

## Rounding top and rounding bottom (saucers)
tags: rounding_top rounding bottom saucer dome slow reversal
A rounding bottom (saucer) is a slow U-shaped turn from falling to rising; Bulkowski ranked it with the lowest failure rate of upward breakouts (+47–48% average rise). Agora's cup-with-handle detector finds rounded bottoms; the rounding top is the dome-shaped mirror, a slow loss of buying that breaks below its two lows.

## Rising and falling channels
tags: ascending_channel descending_channel channel parallel trendlines rising falling
Price moving between two parallel sloping lines. In a rising channel traders buy near the lower line and take profits near the upper; a close below the lower line ends the uptrend's orderly phase. In a falling channel the mirror. A break in the direction of the channel (above a rising channel) is an acceleration that often exhausts.

## Diamond tops and bottoms
tags: diamond top diamond bottom broadening triangle reversal
A broadening formation that turns into a symmetrical triangle, making a diamond shape. Rare. Bulkowski ranked the diamond bottom's downward breakout the best-performing bearish pattern and the diamond top among the weakest upward ones. Agora reads the parts (broadening, triangle) rather than diamonds as such.

## Bump-and-run reversal
tags: bump and run reversal barr bulkowski trend acceleration
Bulkowski's own pattern: a steady trend (the lead-in), then a steep, speculative acceleration away from the trendline (the bump), then a collapse back through the original trendline (the run). The bottom version ranked first in his upward-breakout performance table (+55%). Parabolic moves that break their own steep trendline are its essence.

## Island reversals and gaps
tags: island reversal gaps common gap breakaway gap runaway gap exhaustion gap measuring gap
Edwards and Magee's four gaps: common gaps (inside ranges, usually filled), breakaway gaps (out of a range on heavy volume, starting a move, often never filled), runaway or measuring gaps (mid-trend, on continued volume, often halfway through the move) and exhaustion gaps (late in a trend, on climactic volume, filled within days). An island reversal is a cluster of candles cut off by a gap on each side: the traders on the island are trapped. Which gap it was is only certain afterwards; Kairos flags breakaway gaps and islands as setups.

## Double tops and bottoms: Adam and Eve
tags: adam eve double bottom double top big w big m variants
Bulkowski splits double bottoms by shape: Adam (narrow, V-shaped) and Eve (wide, rounded). Eve & Eve double bottoms performed best (+50% average rise); Adam & Adam worst. A "Big W" or "Big M" has tall sides. Kairos treats them all as doubles; a rounded second bottom is a mild plus.

## High and tight flag
tags: high tight flag htf pole momentum o'neil
A stock that roughly doubles in under two months, then rests in a tight flag that gives back less than 20–25%. William O'Neil's favourite momentum pattern. Bulkowski's book figure (+69% average rise) did not hold in his later study of 2,588 patterns: about a third failed, and the average rise was 27%. Strong poles still matter most.

## Wolfe waves
tags: wolfe wave bill wolfe five wave channel target
Bill and Brian Wolfe's five-wave pattern in a converging channel: point 5 overshoots the line from 1 to 3, and the target is the line from 1 to 4 extended. Bulkowski ranked bull Wolfe waves poorly in bull markets. Subjective to draw; Agora does not detect it.

## Measured moves and failed patterns
tags: measured move failed pattern trap bull trap bear trap failure swing
A measured move expects the second leg of a move to equal the first after a pause. Failed patterns are signals too: when a bull flag or a triangle breaks the "wrong" way, the traders who bought the breakout are trapped and their stops fuel a fast move the other way (a bull trap). Kairos records failures as their own signal ("pattern failed") and learns how they behave.
